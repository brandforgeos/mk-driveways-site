How we think about a garden

We start with how you want to use it

Almost every garden problem is a levels problem wearing a disguise. The patio that puddles, the lawn you cannot mow, the steps that feel wrong underfoot, the retaining wall that leans — all of it comes back to where the ground sits and what is holding it there.

So we start with the survey and the levels, work out the terraces and the falls, then design the surfaces and the planting into that. It is the same order you would build a housing site in, and it produces gardens that work rather than gardens that photograph well for a season.

It also means we are comfortable with the awkward ones — steep gardens, split levels, retaining walls, drainage running the wrong way. Those are the jobs we are actually best at.

The awkward stuff

Retaining, steps and levels

A retaining wall is a structure. It is holding back tonnes of saturated soil and it needs a footing, drainage behind it and the right materials, none of which you can see once the bed is filled.

Steps are the other thing worth getting right. A flight where the rise and going are inconsistent feels wrong immediately and is genuinely a trip hazard. We set out to a consistent 2R + G and build the flight to it, even when the ground would rather we did not.

  • Concrete footings under every retaining structure
  • Drainage gravel and DPM behind the retained face
  • Steps set out to a consistent rise and going throughout
  • Sleeper, block and stone walling — whichever suits the house
  • Falls designed so water leaves the garden, not the house

Questions

Things people ask us

Do you design landscaping schemes as well as build them?
Yes, though we are builders who design rather than a design practice. If your garden needs a planting designer we will say so and work alongside one.
How long does a full garden landscaping project take?
Typically two to four weeks on site for a standard rear garden, longer where there are significant levels, retaining structures or a garden room involved.
Can garden landscaping be done in winter?
Most of it, yes. Groundworks, walling and paving carry on. Turf and planting are better in the shoulder seasons and we will programme around it rather than lay turf into frozen ground.
Do you take away the old garden and spoil when landscaping?
Everything goes by grab lorry as it comes out. No skip on the road and nothing left in a heap at the bottom of the garden.
How much does garden landscaping cost in Milton Keynes?
Most full rear gardens in Milton Keynes come in at £15,000–£35,000, and a simpler refresh such as a new patio and lawn is often £6,000–£12,000. Porcelain patios on their own run about £150–£200 per m² and sandstone £130–£180 per m². Levels, retaining walls and access move the figure more than the choice of paving does, which is why the price is fixed in writing only after the free visit.
Do I need planning permission to landscape my back garden?
Usually not: most rear garden landscaping, including patios, paths, lawns, beds and fences up to 2m, is permitted development. The exceptions are raised decks or platforms over 30cm, significant changes to ground levels, fences over 2m (1m next to a highway), new hard surfaces over 5m² in a front garden that drain to the road, and anything at a listed building or where an Article 4 direction or estate planning condition applies. We check your address at the design visit.
How do you landscape a sloping garden?
A sloping garden is normally landscaped by cutting it into level terraces held by retaining walls, linked by steps, with drainage designed so water leaves the garden rather than heading for the house. Each wall gets a concrete footing and drainage behind it, and every flight of steps is set out to a consistent rise and going so it feels natural underfoot. On a steep plot this structure is usually the biggest single part of the budget.
When does a garden retaining wall need to be designed by an engineer?
As a rule of thumb, a retaining wall holding back more than about a metre of soil, or one with a driveway, building or steep bank above it, should be designed by a structural engineer. Below that it still needs a proper concrete footing, drainage behind the face and a membrane against the soil, because saturated clay pushes hard. A wall on a boundary or close to next door can also raise planning or Party Wall questions, which we flag before design.
How do you fix a waterlogged garden on Milton Keynes clay?
Fixing a waterlogged garden on clay means giving the water somewhere to go: breaking up the compacted layer, importing free-draining topsoil and, where the ground is really bad, laying land drains to a proper outfall. Much of Milton Keynes sits on Oxford Clay, where a simple soakaway often fills and stays full, so we work out where the water can practically and legally discharge before designing anything. Raising beds and lawns slightly helps as well.
What is the difference between hard and soft landscaping?
Hard landscaping is the built structure of a garden — patios, paths, walls, steps, edging, fencing and decking — while soft landscaping is the living part: lawns, beds, topsoil, shrubs, trees and hedging. On a full garden the hard landscaping goes in first because it sets the levels and drainage the planting depends on. It is also where most of the budget goes, and where a poor build is hardest and most expensive to put right.
Should I landscape the garden before or after an extension or garage conversion?
Landscape after the building work wherever you can, because scaffolding, skips, deliveries and drainage connections will damage a finished patio or lawn. What pays to do early is the planning: agree the finished floor level, the patio level against the damp proof course, where the rainwater goes and where ducts for lighting and power cross the garden. If the extension is with us as well, we programme the two so the groundworks are only dug once.
