Homes, ground and conservation in Winslow

Winslow is a brick town: its conservation area appraisal, adopted in April 2026, notes that red brick — mostly in Flemish bond — is prevalent, with render, timber frame, clay tile and some thatch. The core around Market Square, High Street, Sheep Street and Horn Street fronts the pavement, the Victorian and Edwardian streets to the north have front gardens, and modern estates sit around the edge.

The British Geological Survey notes that glacial sand and gravel are widespread around Winslow, alongside clay till and Oxford Clay, so ground can change within a few metres. Sub-base depth and drainage are set from what we find on the day.

Winslow Conservation Area was re-appraised in April 2026 with a revised boundary. There is no Article 4 direction at present, though the appraisal recommends one be considered, and it discourages close-boarded fencing on streets such as Station Road and Avenue Road in favour of traditional boundaries.

Sources: Winslow Conservation Area appraisal (2026).

Council rules and fees for Winslow

  • Dropped kerbs: £520.38 for the vehicular access licence, with planning permission (or a letter confirming none is needed) in place first; built by a private contractor you appoint who holds NRSWA operative and supervisor cards and £10m public liability insurance — the council keeps no list of contractors. Buckinghamshire Council: licence to drop a kerb
  • Front driveways: no planning permission if permeable or drained to your own garden; over 5m² of impermeable surface draining to the road needs it. Planning Portal guidance
  • Planning: householder applications cost £548 (national fee from April 2026); pre-application advice from Buckinghamshire Council costs £579.40 including VAT, for an initial meeting and a written reply.
  • Building regulations: Buckinghamshire Council: building control fees. Buckinghamshire’s building control fees are quoted per project on request.
  • Trees: six weeks’ notice for trees in a conservation area, consent for protected trees — both free. Buckinghamshire Council: work on a protected tree

How much does a driveway cost in Winslow?
For a typical Winslow drive of about 25–45m², a resin bound driveway costs roughly £3,750–£10,000, block paving £3,000–£7,750 and tarmac £2,250–£5,750. A 20–30m² porcelain patio is roughly £3,000–£6,000, and a full rear garden makeover typically £15,000–£35,000.
Which council covers Winslow?
Buckinghamshire Council. A dropped kerb costs £520.38 licence and is built by a private NRSWA-qualified contractor you appoint. Householder planning applications cost £548.

How much does a new driveway cost in Winslow?
The same as anywhere else we work: as a guide, stabilised gravel is £60–£100 per m², tarmac £90–£130, block paving £120–£170 and resin bound £150–£220, all in. See our cost guides for what moves the figure. Your fixed price comes in writing after the free visit.
Do I need permission for a dropped kerb in Winslow?
Yes. The footway belongs to the highway authority, which in Winslow is Buckinghamshire Council. Buckinghamshire Council licenses the crossing (£520.38), and the contractor must hold NRSWA cards and £10 million public liability cover. We prepare the drawings and programme the crossing with the drive. Start the application early — it is usually the longest part.
Do I need planning permission for a new driveway in Winslow?
Generally not, if the surface is permeable — resin bound, gravel or permeable block — or if the water drains to a lawn, border or soakaway on your own land. Over 5m² of non-permeable surface draining onto the road does need permission. Parts of Winslow, particularly the older centre, are in a conservation area, where front boundaries and some materials need more care — worth checking before you plan a change.
What is the ground like in Winslow?
Much of Winslow sits on heavy clay, so water does not soak away easily. Soakaways often struggle, which means the falls and where the water goes need designing rather than assuming — and a permeable surface on clay still needs somewhere for the water to go.
