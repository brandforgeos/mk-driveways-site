## Why scrapping legally matters

When a car comes to the end of its life, the way it is disposed of is controlled by law. Only an Authorised Treatment Facility (ATF) can legally scrap a car. ATFs are licensed sites that deal with the fluids, batteries and other hazardous parts, then recycle what is left.

If a car goes somewhere other than an ATF, you may never get a Certificate of Destruction. Without that document, the DVLA may still have you down as the keeper. That can leave you open to fines, tax reminders or worse if the car turns up somewhere it shouldn't.

Doing it properly is not complicated. It mostly comes down to choosing the right buyer and keeping the right paperwork.

## What a Certificate of Destruction is

A Certificate of Destruction, often shortened to CoD, is the official record that a vehicle has reached the end of its life and has been taken in for scrapping. It is issued by an Authorised Treatment Facility (ATF), which is the only kind of site that can legally scrap a car in the UK.

The CoD is the end point of the process. It confirms the car has gone to the right place and that the DVLA has been told. From that point, the car is off the road for good.

## Who issues it

Only an ATF can issue a Certificate of Destruction. A scrap car collector, like us, buys the car from you and takes it to an ATF. The ATF then deals with the fluids, batteries and hazardous parts, recycles the rest, issues the CoD and notifies the DVLA.

This is why it matters who you sell to. If a car ends up somewhere other than an ATF, no CoD is issued, and the DVLA may still think the car is yours.

## When you get it

The CoD is normally issued within 7 days of the car arriving at the ATF. The DVLA is told at the same time, which is what triggers the automatic refund of any full months of vehicle tax left on the car.

You don't need to apply for anything. As long as the car goes to an ATF, the certificate follows.

## Why the Certificate of Destruction matters

The CoD is your proof that you are no longer responsible for the vehicle. That matters for a few reasons:

- **Liability.** If the car were ever used or found somewhere it shouldn't be, the CoD shows it was scrapped and was no longer yours.
- **DVLA records.** It confirms the DVLA has been told, so you shouldn't receive tax reminders or keeper letters for the car.
- **Tax refund.** The DVLA refunds any full months of vehicle tax once it knows the car has been scrapped.
- **Peace of mind.** It is a clear end to the story. The car is gone, properly and legally.

Keep it somewhere safe, along with the yellow slip from your V5C.

## What the yellow slip is for

When you hand over the V5C logbook, keep the small yellow slip, the section for selling to the motor trade. It is your record of the handover until the CoD arrives. Once you have the certificate, keep both together.

If you don't have a V5C at all, you can still scrap the car with proof of ID and proof of ownership and address. Our guide on [scrapping a car with no logbook](/guides/scrap-car-without-logbook) explains how.

## What to do if you don't receive a CoD

Most of the time, the process runs smoothly and there is nothing to chase. If you haven't heard anything after a reasonable wait beyond the normal 7 days, take these steps:

1. **Check your details.** Make sure the name and address you gave matched your ID and the V5C. A small error can slow things down.
2. **Contact the buyer.** Get in touch with whoever collected the car. If you sold to us, call or WhatsApp 07598 314366, or email info@webuyscrapcars-corby.co.uk, and we will look into it.
3. **Check for a tax refund.** If the DVLA has refunded your remaining tax, that is a sign the scrapping has been recorded.
4. **Contact the DVLA.** If you still have doubts, the DVLA can confirm whether the vehicle is recorded as scrapped.
5. **Keep your records.** Hold on to the yellow slip, any messages from the buyer and your bank transfer record.

## Signs a buyer may not provide a CoD

There are a few warning signs that a car may not be going to an ATF:

- The buyer offers to pay in cash, which is illegal under the Scrap Metal Dealers Act 2013.
- They don't ask for ID or proof of address, which a legal buyer must check.
- They are vague about where the car is going.
- They can't confirm they hold a waste carrier licence and a scrap metal collector's licence.

We hold both licences, we check ID as the law requires, we pay by bank transfer, and every car goes to a licensed ATF.

## Is a Certificate of Destruction the same as a V5C?

No. The V5C is the registration document that shows who the registered keeper is while the car is in use. The Certificate of Destruction is issued at the end of the car's life to show it has been scrapped. You hand over the V5C when you sell the car, and the CoD is what confirms the story is finished.

## What the CoD doesn't cover

The certificate deals with the vehicle record. It doesn't cancel your insurance, so you need to do that yourself once the car has gone. It also doesn't deal with any toll, parking or congestion accounts linked to the registration, so cancel those too. And it can't bring back a personalised number plate. If you want to keep a private plate, you must transfer or retain it with the DVLA before the car is scrapped.

## Does every scrapped car get a CoD?

Every car scrapped at an ATF should. That covers cars, and vans up to 3.5 tonnes. Whether your car is a running car, an [MOT failure](/mot-failure), a [non-runner](/non-runner) or a [damaged car or write-off](/damaged-cars), the same rule applies.

## A note on cash buyers

Cash and paperwork tend to go together. A buyer who follows the rules on payment usually follows the rules on paperwork too. Because paying cash for scrap metal has been illegal since the Scrap Metal Dealers Act 2013, an offer of cash is a strong sign the car may not reach an ATF and no certificate will follow.

## Can I scrap a car without a V5C?

Yes. Missing logbooks are common. They get lost in house moves, thrown out by mistake, or were simply never passed on when the car changed hands. Not having the V5C doesn't stop you scrapping a car legally.

What it does mean is that you need other evidence. A scrap car buyer has to be satisfied that the person selling is who they say they are and has the right to sell the car.

## What the V5C normally does

The V5C logbook records the registered keeper of the vehicle. When a car is scrapped with its logbook, you hand the V5C over and keep the small yellow slip, the section for selling to the motor trade. The ATF then issues a Certificate of Destruction and tells the DVLA.

Without the V5C, those last steps still happen. The difference is in the checks at the start.

## What you need instead

To scrap a car with no logbook, you will need:

- **Photo ID.** A driving licence or passport.
- **Proof of address.** A recent document showing your name and address.
- **Proof of ownership.** Something that links you to the car.

Proof of ID and address is needed for every sale, logbook or not, because by law we must check the seller's name and address. The extra piece without a V5C is showing that the car is yours to sell.

## What counts as proof of ownership

Proof of ownership is anything that reasonably connects you to the vehicle. Depending on your situation, that might include paperwork from when you bought the car, service or repair records in your name, or insurance documents for the car. If you are not sure whether what you have is enough, call or WhatsApp us on 07598 314366 before you book and we will talk it through.

## The question behind the question

Most people ask whether to scrap or repair at a specific moment. The car has failed its MOT, a garage has handed over a quote, or the car simply won't start one morning. The real question is whether spending money on this car makes more sense than letting it go.

There is no single right answer for every car. But there are some clear signs that point one way or the other, and this guide sets them out.

## The basic rule

If the repairs cost more than the car will be worth once it is fixed, scrapping is often the sensible option. That is the starting point for any decision.

To use that rule, you need two figures: the repair quote from a garage, and a realistic idea of what the car is worth in good order. It also helps to know its scrap value, which you can see in a couple of minutes on our [instant price page](/get-a-price).

## Scrap or repair: a quick comparison

| Situation | Leans towards | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minor MOT failure such as bulbs, wipers or a tyre | Repair | Cheap fixes that keep a working car on the road |
| Structural rust on sills or subframes | Scrap | Often costly to fix properly and tends to spread |
| Gearbox failure | Scrap, for most older cars | Replacement or rebuild can outweigh the car's value |
| Worn clutch on an otherwise good car | Depends | Worth fixing on a newer car, less so on an old one |
| Head gasket failure | Scrap, for most older cars | Labour-heavy repair with a risk of further damage |
| Long list of advisories | Consider scrapping | Today's advisories are often next year's failures |
| Insurance write-off you kept | Depends on category | Cat S and Cat N can be repaired. Cat A and Cat B cannot return to the road |
| Car not used and sitting for years | Often scrap | Flat tyres, seized brakes and perished parts add up |

## Questions to ask yourself

Before you decide, it helps to answer a few honest questions:

1. **How much is the repair?** Get a written quote and ask whether anything else is likely to be found once work starts.
2. **What is the car worth when it is fixed?** Look at what similar cars sell for in good condition.
3. **What else is due soon?** Tyres, brakes, timing belts and advisories all add up over the next year.
4. **How much do you rely on it?** A car you need every day is worth more to you than one that sits.
5. **Would the money be better put towards something else?** Sometimes the repair bill is better spent on a replacement.

## When repairing makes sense

Repair is usually the better choice when the car is in good overall condition, the fault is isolated and the cost is modest compared with what the car is worth. A newer car with low mileage that has failed on a cheap item is a good example.

It is also worth repairing if the car has a value beyond the numbers to you, and you are happy with the cost.

## When scrapping makes sense

Scrapping tends to be the better choice when the repair bill is larger than the car's value, the car has more than one serious fault, or you can see a series of bills coming one after another. Structural rust, gearbox problems, a worn clutch on an old car and head gasket failure are typical tipping points.

A car that has been off the road for a long time also tends to need more work than it first appears. See our [non-runner](/non-runner) page if your car has been sitting.

## What about write-offs?

If an insurer has written off your car and you kept it, the category matters:

- Cat A and Cat B cars cannot return to the road.
- Cat S cars have structural damage but can be repaired.
- Cat N cars have non-structural damage and can be repaired.

For Cat S and Cat N, the same repair-versus-value test applies. Our [damaged cars](/damaged-cars) page explains how we handle write-offs and buy-backs.

## Hidden costs of keeping an old car

The repair quote is rarely the only cost. An older car may need tyres, brakes, a battery or a cambelt in the months ahead. Insurance, tax and the next MOT all keep coming. When you add those up, a car that looks worth saving on one bill can look different over a full year.

On the other hand, replacing a car has costs too. Be fair to both sides when you do the sums.

## Selling privately instead

If the car is worth repairing but you don't want to do it yourself, selling it privately with the fault declared is another option. That tends to suit newer cars with a single, clear problem. For older cars with several faults, private buyers are harder to find, and scrapping is usually simpler.

## Getting a second opinion

If a garage quote seems high, it is reasonable to ask another garage to look at the same fault. Make sure you are comparing like for like, including parts and labour. If both quotes are well above the car's value, that is usually a clear answer.

## The short version

A scrap car is valued mainly as a source of recyclable metal. Scrap metal is bought and sold by the tonne, so the starting point for any scrap price is how much the car weighs and what the market is paying per tonne right now.

From there, the price is adjusted for the details of the car: whether it is complete, whether the catalytic converter is still fitted, whether there are keys and a V5C, and where it is. That is the whole picture.

## Weight: the biggest factor

Weight is where most of a scrap car's value comes from. Steel, aluminium and other metals make up the bulk of a car, and the heavier the car, the more of them there are.

That is why a large estate, people carrier, 4x4 or van tends to be worth more as scrap than a small hatchback. It is not about how nice the car is. It is about how much metal it carries.

## The scrap metal market

The price paid per tonne for scrap metal is not fixed. It moves with supply and demand in the wider metals market, and it can rise or fall over weeks and months.

This means the same car can be worth a little more or a little less depending on when it is scrapped. It also explains why a price a friend got some time ago may not match what you are offered today. Our [instant price](/get-a-price) reflects current conditions, not an old figure.

## Make and model

Make and model matter mainly because they tell us the car's weight and construction. Two cars of a similar size can be built quite differently, with different amounts of metal. When we look up your registration, we can see these details, which is how the online price can be specific to your car.

## Completeness

A complete car has its engine, gearbox, wheels, panels and catalytic converter. Each of those adds weight or value. When parts are missing, there is less to recycle, so the price drops.

This is the second most common reason for a lower figure, after a car simply being light. If parts have been removed, tell George when he calls to book, and he will talk it through before collection.

## The catalytic converter

The catalytic converter deserves its own mention. It contains precious metals that are valuable to recyclers. A car with its converter fitted is worth noticeably more as scrap than the same car without one.

Converters are sometimes stolen from parked cars. If yours has been taken, tell us when you get your price.

## Keys and V5C

Keys make a car easier to move and handle during loading, especially if the steering lock needs releasing. The V5C logbook keeps the paperwork simple. Neither is essential, but both help. You can still sell a car without the V5C if you have photo ID and proof of ownership and address. Our guide on [scrapping a car without a logbook](/guides/scrap-car-without-logbook) explains how.

## Location

Where the car is affects the cost of collecting it, so the postcode is part of how the price is worked out. Collection is always free to you. We cover Corby, Kettering and the towns and villages around them, and further afield by arrangement.

## What doesn't affect the price much

Some things matter less than people expect:

- **Whether the car runs.** A non-runner weighs the same as a car that starts. See our [non-runner cost guide](/costs/non-runner).
- **Cosmetic damage.** Dents and scratches don't change the metal content.
- **Mileage.** A high mileage car is valued on its weight like any other.
- **MOT status.** An expired MOT or a failure rarely changes a scrap figure. See our [MOT failure cost guide](/costs/mot-failure).

## Old cars versus newer cars

Age matters only as far as it affects weight and completeness. An older car is not automatically worth less as scrap than a newer one of the same size. A complete twenty-year-old estate can easily be worth more as scrap than a small, newer hatchback, because the estate simply carries more metal.

## Vans and 4x4s

The same logic applies to vans, 4x4s and pickups, but they are usually heavier than cars, so weight plays an even bigger role. Size, body type and payload rating all hint at how much metal a van carries. Our [scrap van cost guide](/costs/scrap-van) goes into this in detail.
