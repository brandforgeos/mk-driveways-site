## The first 60 seconds

Most breakdowns give you some warning: a warning light, a loss of power, a strange noise or a shudder. The moment you notice something wrong, your only job is to get the car somewhere safer while it still moves.

1. **Indicate and move left.** Aim for a hard shoulder, lay-by, verge, side road or car park. Even a few hundred yards of coasting can get you out of a live lane.
2. **Hazard lights on** as soon as you stop.
3. **Turn the wheels slightly towards the verge** and put the handbrake on. If the car is hit from behind, it is less likely to roll into traffic.
4. **Sidelights on** if it is dark or visibility is poor.

Don't try to keep driving to "get home" if the car is overheating, has lost oil pressure or is making a grinding noise. A short drive can turn a cheap repair into an expensive one.

## Where to wait: it depends on the road

### Motorways and fast dual carriageways

Get everyone out through the doors on the side away from traffic. Climb over the barrier if there is one and stand well back from the road, up the bank if possible. Wear a high-vis vest if you have one. Don't put a warning triangle out on a motorway or fast dual carriageway: walking along the road to place it is too dangerous. Our [A14 breakdown advice](/guides/a14-breakdown-advice) covers fast roads in more detail.

If you have stopped in a live lane and can't get out safely, stay in the car with your seatbelt on and hazards on, and call 999.

### Single-carriageway A roads and country lanes

Pull onto the verge or into a gateway or lay-by if you can. Stand well away from the car, behind it and off the road, where approaching drivers can see you. Bends and hill brows are the danger points, because drivers come round them with little warning. If you carry a warning triangle and it is safe to do so, place it at least 45 metres behind the car on the same side of the road.

### Town streets, estates and car parks

You are usually safe to wait in or beside the car, as long as it is parked out of the flow of traffic. If you have stopped at a junction or on a roundabout, get out and wait on the pavement. If the car is light enough and the road is quiet, you may be able to push it a short way into a safer spot, but only with help and only when there's no traffic.

## Children and pets

Children should get out on the side away from traffic, with an adult, and wait behind the barrier or well away from the road. Keep small children's hands held. On a quieter street where it's safe to stay in the car, keep doors locked and seatbelts on.

Pets should usually stay in the car on fast roads. A frightened dog loose near traffic is a danger to itself and to drivers. The exception is if the car is at risk, such as from smoke or fire. Then get the animal out on a lead. On hot days, don't leave pets in a closed car: open windows and wait nearby where it is safe. Tell us about pets and children when you call so we can plan the journey.

## Breaking down at night

At night, other drivers see you later, so visibility matters even more.

- Keep hazards and sidelights on.
- Wear a high-vis vest or light-coloured clothing if you have it.
- Use your phone torch to see where you are walking, not to signal traffic.
- Stay behind the barrier or well off the road, not in front of the car.

If you feel unsafe because of where you are or who is around, lock yourself in the car with hazards on if it's out of traffic, and tell us when you call. If you feel in danger, call 999. We are open 24/7, so a breakdown at 2am gets the same response as one at 2pm.

## After an accident

If you have been in a collision, the order of priorities is people first, then paperwork, then the car.

1. **Check for injuries.** If anyone is hurt, or the road is blocked, call 999.
2. **Get to safety** using the same rules as a breakdown.
3. **Exchange details** with the other driver: name, address, phone, registration and insurance details. By law, you must stop and give details if anyone is injured or another vehicle or property is damaged.
4. **Take photos** of the vehicles, the road and any damage, if it is safe.
5. **Report it to the police within 24 hours** if you couldn't exchange details at the scene or someone was injured.
6. **Tell your insurer** as soon as you can.

We carry out accident recovery and work with insurance and accident management companies. We can take a damaged car to a garage, your home or our secure storage yard while the claim is sorted. Some of our trucks can carry one car and tow a second, which helps when two vehicles need moving. Don't open a hot radiator cap or restart an engine that has stalled in floodwater: both can cause injury or serious damage.

## Why the A14 needs extra care

The A14 runs past Kettering and is a busy freight route. Much of the traffic is heavy goods vehicles travelling at speed, and like many fast dual carriageways, it doesn't always give you a hard shoulder to stop on. The same goes for other fast dual carriageways around Corby and Kettering, and for busy A roads like the A43 and A6003.

On roads like these, a stationary car is at real risk. Drivers behind may not realise you have stopped until they are very close. That is why the advice for fast roads is different from what you would do on a town street. This guide focuses on fast roads. For general advice, read [what to do if you break down](/guides/what-to-do-if-you-break-down).

## If you feel the car going wrong

Act early. The moment a warning light comes on, the engine loses power, or you feel a tyre going, start moving left.

1. **Indicate left and get into the inside lane.**
2. **Look for a way off:** an exit slip road, a lay-by, an emergency area or a wide verge. Leaving the main carriageway is always the best result.
3. **If you can't reach one,** pull as far left onto the verge as you safely can.
4. **Hazard lights on** as soon as you stop, and sidelights too if it is dark or visibility is poor.
5. **Turn the wheels towards the verge** and put the handbrake on.

Don't stop on the central reservation side unless you have no choice. Being trapped between two lanes of fast traffic is one of the most dangerous places to be.

## Getting out and waiting safely

Once you have stopped:

- **Get everyone out on the left**, the side away from traffic. Children and pets go with an adult, pets on a lead if they have to come out.
- **Climb over the barrier** if there is one and stand well back, up the bank if you can.
- **Wear a high-vis vest** if you have one.
- **Don't stand in front of or behind the car.** If something hits it, that is where it will go.
- **Don't try any repairs**, even changing a tyre. On a fast road, the risk isn't worth it.
- **Don't place a warning triangle.** Walking along a fast carriageway to put one out is too dangerous.

## If you can't get out of the live lane

Sometimes a car dies suddenly and there's no way to get across. If you are stopped in a live lane and can't get out safely, stay in the car with your seatbelt on and your hazards on, and call 999 straight away. The police can arrange for the road to be protected. Once you are safe, call us.

## How to describe where you are

On a long dual carriageway with few landmarks, an accurate location saves real time. The best ways, in order:

1. **WhatsApp live location** to 07598 314366. We see exactly where you are and which side of the road.
2. **what3words**, if you have the app.
3. **Marker posts.** Small posts along the verge of many major roads carry a reference number. Read the number off the nearest post and tell us.
4. **Junctions and direction.** Tell us the last junction or exit you passed, the next one you were heading for and your direction of travel, eastbound or westbound.

Which carriageway you are on matters as much as where you are. A truck on the wrong side can lose a lot of time getting turned round.

## Start with what you hear and see

The way your car behaves when you turn the key or press the start button is the best clue. Before anything else, check the simple things: is the car in park or neutral, is your foot on the clutch or brake if the car needs it, and is the key fob battery working.

| What happens | Most likely cause |
| --- | --- |
| Nothing at all, no dash lights | Very flat battery or a loose or corroded terminal |
| Dash lights come on but dim or flicker, clicking noise | Flat or weak battery |
| Single loud click, lights stay bright | Starter motor or its connection |
| Engine turns slowly, struggles, then stops | Weak battery, often in cold weather |
| Engine turns over normally but won't fire | Fuel, ignition or engine management problem |
| Starts then dies straight away | Fuel supply, immobiliser or sensor fault |

## Flat battery: the usual suspect

A flat battery is the most common reason a car won't start. Common causes are:

- **Lights or a phone charger left on.**
- **Short journeys.** The alternator doesn't get long enough to recharge the battery, especially in winter with heaters, wipers and lights on.
- **Cold weather.** Batteries lose power in the cold, and engines need more power to turn over. The first frosty morning of the year catches out a lot of drivers.
- **Cars left unused.** Even parked and locked, modern cars draw a little power for alarms and electronics. A car left for a few weeks can go flat.
- **Age.** Batteries wear out over time. If yours is several years old and struggling, it may be at the end of its life.

## Starter motor problems

If the dash lights are bright but you get a single click or nothing when you try to start, the battery may be fine and the starter motor may be at fault. A jump start won't fix a failed starter. The car will need a garage, and we can [recover it](/breakdown-recovery) there for you.

## Fuel and ignition problems

If the engine turns over at normal speed but won't catch, the battery is doing its job. Check you have fuel: a faulty gauge or running lower than you thought is more common than you might think. Beyond that, the problem could be fuel delivery, ignition or an engine management fault. Warning lights on the dash can help a garage diagnose it.

Don't keep cranking the engine for long periods. It drains the battery and can overheat the starter motor. A few short attempts with a pause between them is enough.

## When a jump start will work, and when it won't

A jump start works when the battery is flat but healthy enough to recharge, for example after lights were left on. If the car starts and runs normally after a jump, drive it for a decent run, ideally 30 minutes or more on faster roads, to recharge the battery.

A jump start is only a short-term fix if:

- The battery goes flat again the next day.
- The car was jump started recently and has gone flat again.
- The battery is old or has been flat for weeks.
- The engine turns over slowly even with a jump.

In those cases, the battery probably needs replacing, or there's a charging problem. We can supply and fit a new battery on the spot, priced by the battery your car needs and confirmed before fitting. See the [jump start and battery cost guide](/costs/jump-start).

## Stop-start cars: AGM and EFB batteries

Many newer cars have stop-start, which switches the engine off at traffic lights. These cars need a more robust battery, usually an **AGM** (absorbent glass mat) or **EFB** (enhanced flooded battery). Fitting an ordinary battery to a stop-start car can mean poor performance and a short battery life.

Some cars also need the new battery registered with the car's computer so the charging system knows it has been replaced. When you call, tell us the make, model and year so we bring the right type.

## Safe jump starting basics

If you are jump starting with another car and leads, take care:

1. **Check the leads and both batteries** for damage, leaks or a bulging case. Don't jump a damaged battery.
2. **Both engines off.** Keep the cars close but not touching.
3. **Red lead to the positive (+) terminal** of the flat battery, then to the positive terminal of the good battery.
4. **Black lead to the negative (-) terminal** of the good battery, then to a bare metal earth point on the car with the flat battery, away from the battery itself. Many cars have a marked jump-start point; check the handbook.
5. **Start the good car** and let it run for a few minutes, then try the flat car.
6. **Remove the leads in reverse order** once it starts.

Never let the clamps touch each other while connected. If you are not confident, or the car has a complex electrical system, it is safer to call us. We use proper jump packs designed for the job.

## Cold weather tips

- Turn off lights, heaters, rear demister and wipers before starting.
- Give the battery a rest between attempts.
- If the car has been sitting unused through a cold spell, expect a weak battery.
- If the battery is several years old, consider replacing it before winter rather than waiting for it to fail.

## Cars that have been left unused

A car parked for weeks, perhaps while you were away or working from home, often won't start. A jump start may get it going, but the battery may not recover fully if it has been deeply flat. Tyres can also lose pressure over time. Check them before you set off.

## Why doing it properly matters

When a car reaches the end of its life, it has to be scrapped at an **Authorised Treatment Facility (ATF)**. ATFs are licensed sites that safely remove fluids, batteries and hazardous parts, and recycle the rest. Only an ATF can issue the Certificate of Destruction that officially ends the vehicle's life.

If you hand your car to someone who isn't licensed, you could still be the registered keeper. That means you could be liable for fines, tax or anything else linked to that registration. Doing it the right way takes very little effort.

## Step by step: scrapping a car in England

1. **Choose a licensed collector or ATF.** Anyone collecting scrap vehicles should hold a waste carrier licence and, as a scrap metal collector, a scrap metal licence from the local council. Rapid Recovery holds both.
2. **Get a price.** Tell us the make, model, year and condition. We confirm a price before collection.
3. **Remove your belongings** and any private number plate you want to keep (see below).
4. **We collect the car,** free of charge, from your home, driveway or roadside, whether it runs or not.
5. **The car goes to an ATF.** The ATF issues a **Certificate of Destruction** and notifies the DVLA.
6. **You are paid by bank transfer.** Cash payments for scrap are illegal.
7. **The DVLA confirms** the change, and any full months of tax are refunded automatically.

## What is a Certificate of Destruction?

A Certificate of Destruction (CoD) is the official proof that your vehicle has been scrapped at an ATF. It shows you are no longer responsible for it. The ATF issues it and notifies the DVLA. Keep a copy of any paperwork for your records.

## Telling the DVLA and the V5C logbook

When your car is scrapped, the DVLA needs to know. The ATF notifies the DVLA, but you also have a part to play with your V5C (the vehicle log book).

- Give the V5C to the ATF or the licensed collector taking the car there.
- Keep the yellow section of the V5C, the part used when a vehicle goes to the motor trade.
- Tell the DVLA that the car has gone to a scrapyard or ATF. You can do this online, or by post using that yellow section.

The DVLA then sends you a letter confirming you are no longer the keeper. If you don't receive it within a few weeks, contact the DVLA.

## Can I scrap a car without a logbook?

Yes. A missing V5C doesn't stop you scrapping a car. We can collect a car with no V5C as long as you can show proof of ID and that you are entitled to dispose of it. You can still tell the DVLA that the car has been scrapped.

## Will I get a tax refund?

Vehicle tax isn't transferable, and it is cancelled when the DVLA learns the car has been scrapped. The DVLA refunds any full calendar months of tax left automatically. You don't need to apply. Part months are not refunded. If you pay by Direct Debit, it is cancelled automatically too.

## Do I need to SORN a car I'm scrapping?

Not after it has been scrapped. Once the DVLA has been told the car has gone to an ATF, it is off the road permanently and you aren't responsible for it. If the car is sitting off the road waiting for collection, it must either be taxed or declared SORN until it is scrapped. An untaxed car must not be parked on a public road.

## Why can't I be paid in cash?

The **Scrap Metal Dealers Act 2013** bans cash payments for scrap metal in England and Wales, including scrap cars. Payment must be by bank transfer or cheque so it can be traced. We always pay by bank transfer. Be wary of anyone offering cash for a scrap car: they are breaking the law, and it is a warning sign about how they will deal with your vehicle.

## What affects how much my scrap car is worth?

- **Weight.** Most of a scrap car's value comes from its metal, so heavier vehicles are usually worth more.
- **Scrap metal prices,** which go up and down with the market.
- **Make and model.** Some vehicles have parts in demand.
- **Completeness.** A car missing its engine, catalytic converter or wheels is worth less.
- **Condition.** Accident damage or a non-running engine affects the value, but we still take them.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Selling to an unlicensed buyer** for a slightly higher price. If the car isn't taken to an ATF, you may never get a Certificate of Destruction and could stay liable as the keeper.
- **Leaving the car on the street untaxed** while you wait for collection. Keep it taxed, or SORN it and keep it off the road.
- **Forgetting the private plate** until after the car has gone.
- **Not cancelling insurance**, which means paying for a car you no longer own.

## Before we collect: a short checklist

- **Personal items.** Check the glovebox, door pockets, under seats, the boot and the spare wheel well. Don't forget car park passes, toll tags, sunglasses and child seats.
- **Private number plates.** If your car has a private plate you want to keep, you must transfer it or put it on retention with the DVLA **before** the car is scrapped. Once the car has been destroyed, the registration may be lost.
- **Insurance.** Cancel your insurance once the car is collected. You may be due a refund.
- **Finance.** If the car is on finance, you can't scrap it until the finance is settled, because it isn't legally yours.
- **Keys and V5C.** Have them ready if you have them.
